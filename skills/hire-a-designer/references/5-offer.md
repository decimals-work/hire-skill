# Stage 5: land the offer

If you've made it this far: congrats. You're close to the finish line. But you still have to make the case for why this is the best opportunity for this person's career.

More often than not, the best candidates are at or above the top of your desired pay range. If they're worth it, pay it. If you genuinely can't, it's on you to get creative.

Remember the friction from the work trial? This is a real one: handle it the way you'd want to work together.

**What it's for:** an offer isn't a number you send. It's the moment the candidate finds out whether you meant everything you said.

## Method

### 1. Find out what they care about

Ask, don't guess. In the last conversations, find out what matters most to them in this move: money, title, scope, flexibility, learning, the team, side projects, timing. Write it in `templates/offer-plan.md`.

### 2. Be open before they have to ask

Be forthright about the company's growth and how the equity works, so the candidate doesn't have to press for details.

### 3. Know your room

With the hiring manager, write down:

- The range, and whether you can go to the top of it, or above, for this person. If not, why not, and who could change that.
- **What else you can offer:** salary is only one of the variables to negotiate. Sign-on bonuses are usually an easy win. Sometimes even non-financial incentives can go far. We've seen offers land with things like remote work flexibility, an education stipend and the ability to keep working on side projects.
- Where you'd stop, and what you'd do then.

### 4. Make it clear you're on the same page

Figure out what the designer cares about and make sure it's clear that you're on the same page. The hiring manager makes the case in their own words: why this person, from their work and the trial.

### 5. Handle the friction

When the candidate comes back with a counter, that's the friction point. Respond quickly, explain your reasoning and look for an answer that works on both sides. How you handle it is what they'll expect working with you to be like.

## Borrowed from negotiators: interests, not positions

In *Getting to Yes*, Roger Fisher and William Ury separate positions ("I need $X") from the interests behind them (security, feeling valued, a mortgage, wanting to learn). Two sides can often meet on interests when they can't on a number. Ask "what would make this an easy yes?" and listen for the interest. A sign-on bonus might meet a one-off need that a salary change can't.

## Checks

1. The offer plan has: what the candidate cares about (from them), the range and whether you can go above it, the options beyond salary, where you'd stop, and who makes the call.
2. The cold read in `judges.md` ("Offer"). This one has the candidate's details in it: run it with a fresh model, never by sending it to someone outside the company.

## Hand over

`hire/offer.md`: the offer plan and the points the hiring manager wants to make, in their words.
